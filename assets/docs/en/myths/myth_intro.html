The ancient Egyptians sought to understand everything they observed: the course of the sun, the Nile flood, death, the power of the king. Myths gave them these explanations. They did not form a single, fixed book: they survive in fragments in the texts of pyramids and tombs, in hymns, temple rituals and magical spells, and they vary from one period and one city to another.

Several great stories shed particular light on Egyptian thought:

  • the myth of the day cycle follows the journey of the sun, which crosses the sky by day, then faces the forces of chaos every night before being reborn in the morning;
  • the Osirian myth tells of the reign of Osiris, his murder by his brother Seth and the struggle of his son Horus to win back the throne;
  • the myth of death describes the journey of the deceased to the afterlife, from embalming to judgement before Osiris;
  • the Distant Goddess tells of the flight of the Eye of Re, a raging lioness gone to Nubia, and of her calmed return with the flood;
  • the Famine Stela relates how King Djoser, after seven years without a flood, obtained from the god Khnum the return of the Nile waters.
