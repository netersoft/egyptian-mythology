To understand the importance of the cult of the dead, it helps to know the Osirian myth first. In ancient Egypt, death was a passing rather than an end: it marked a transition to something else. As a stage of existence, death was surrounded by a number of rites which belong directly to the Osirian myth, and for the ancient Egyptians the ceremonies and beliefs surrounding death made up an important part of life. The Egyptians believed that after death the soul of the deceased could be reborn and enter the "kingdom of the dead" and eternal rest. Several stages prepared the deceased for the afterlife, and the myth of death can be divided into two parts:

- the first, the journey of the deceased to the afterlife, which begins with the embalming ceremony;
- the second, the judgment of the deceased by the god Osiris on reaching the afterlife, which may grant them eternal rest.

Embalming

Embalming is a ritual performed on earth by priests. For the Egyptians, a human being was made up of five elements: the djet, the physical body; the shut, the shadow; the ren, the name; the ba, which could be translated as the soul; and the ka, a kind of vital force. The ka is what truly makes a person: it is born at the same time as them and survives them after death. The ka, together with the soul of the deceased, the ba, continues the journey to the afterlife. For the ka and the ba to be able to leave the body and join the kingdom of the dead, the djet, the body, has to be preserved. To this end, the priests perform an embalming ceremony that turns the body of the deceased into a mummy. Mummification purifies the body and protects it from decay, the aim being to make the body of the deceased resemble that of Osiris, who was himself mummified so as to live on for eternity.
Mummification lasts seventy days and is carried out by the utyu, the priests in charge of embalming. The body is first washed, then dried in the sun and coated with oils and beeswax, whose antibacterial properties help preserve it. It is worth noting that the preservation of bodies in Egypt was naturally helped by the sandy soil and the dry climate. Some organs, such as the lungs, stomach, intestines and liver, are removed and placed in canopic jars bearing the likenesses of the four sons of Horus. The heart, seat of thought and feeling, is usually left in place. The brain, on the other hand, is drawn out through the nose with a hook: it had no value in the Egyptian mind, since the heart was the seat of thought, which is probably why it was removed and sometimes replaced with beeswax. Once all the organs have been taken out, the body is packed in natron (a salt found naturally in Egypt's salt lakes), which draws the moisture and fat out of the body and, together with Egypt's dry climate, preserves it. The natron, however, leaves the body shrunken and flattened, so linen is packed inside it and into the eye sockets to restore its shape. Once the body is ready, the utyu begin the wrapping. Amulets are placed on the body of the deceased, which is then wound in strips of linen provided by the family. For the wealthiest, a funerary mask completes the whole. The mummy is then placed in a coffin and buried according to the social rank and wealth of the deceased.
If the body is not embalmed, the djet becomes the khat after death and cannot reach eternal rest. The embalming ritual was created by Isis, with the help of Anubis, when she reassembled the body of her husband Osiris to bring him back to life. The statues and offerings placed beside the deceased in the tomb accompany them on their way to the judgment of the soul.

The judgment of the soul

Once the djet has been prepared and preserved, the deceased can begin the journey to the afterlife. The ba, the soul of the deceased, and the ka, their vital force, leave the body, to which they can return because the djet has been preserved, and set out for the kingdom of the dead. But the kingdom of the dead is not open to everyone: the deceased must pass through many stages, and at each one recite a spell written in the Book of the Dead. This book, made up of several papyrus scrolls, charts the journey of the deceased to the afterlife. Legend credits its writing to the god Thoth, who is called upon to cut off the heads of all who stand in the way of the deceased on the road to the kingdom of the dead. The best-known episode of the Book of the Dead, because of its central importance, is the weighing of the heart.

In the weighing of the heart, or psychostasia, the heart of the deceased, guided by Anubis through the passage between life and death, is placed on one pan of a scale, with a feather (representing the goddess Maat) on the other. If the heart is lighter (meaning that it is not weighed down by wrongdoing), the deceased may enter the kingdom of the dead, where Osiris awaits, and begin a new life there. Otherwise, the heart is devoured by a monster (usually the goddess Amemet, with the head of a crocodile, the body of a lion and the hindquarters of a hippopotamus), and the soul is lost forever. Horus presides over the judgment and Thoth records the result. It is the heart that is weighed to judge the soul, because the heart was believed to be incapable of lying: every wrong the deceased committed in life is recorded within it.
Osiris himself only became god of the kingdom of the dead after successfully passing the weighing of the heart, which is why the dead sought to identify with Osiris in order to reach the kingdom of the dead and rest in peace.
