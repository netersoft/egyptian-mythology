For the Egyptians, death was not an end but a passage to another life. This passage was by no means automatic, however: the body had to be preserved, the right rites performed and the ordeal of judgement passed. The whole journey is modelled on the story of Osiris, the first god to be mummified and brought back to life, who became the ruler of the dead. Every deceased person hoped to follow his example and become, in turn, “an Osiris”.

The parts of the self

The Egyptians believed that a human being was made up of several elements, which death separated:

  • the djet, the body, which embalming was meant to preserve;
  • the ka, the life force, born with the individual and outliving them, fed by the offerings left in the tomb;
  • the ba, most often translated as “the soul”, shown as a bird with a human head, free to leave the tomb by day and return to it at night;
  • the ren, the name: as long as it was spoken, the deceased went on existing;
  • the shuyet, the shadow, inseparable from the body;
  • the ib, the heart, seat of thought, memory and moral conscience.

After a favourable judgement, the ka and the ba came together to form the akh, the shining, immortal spirit of the justified dead.

Embalming

So that the ka and the ba could return to the deceased, the body had to be kept intact. Embalming, which lasted seventy days, was entrusted to specialised priests. The chief embalmer wore a jackal mask: he embodied Anubis, the god who had mummified Osiris.

The body was first washed. The brain, to which the Egyptians attached no importance, was drawn out through the nose with a hook. Through an incision in the left side, the embalmers then removed the internal organs. The liver, lungs, stomach and intestines were dried and placed in four canopic jars, under the protection of the four sons of Horus: human-headed Imsety guarded the liver, baboon-headed Hapy the lungs, jackal-headed Duamutef the stomach and falcon-headed Qebehsenuef the intestines. The heart, seat of thought, stayed in place: the deceased would need it at the judgement.

The body was then covered with natron, a natural salt taken from dried-up lakes, which dried it out for about forty days. It was then stuffed with linen and resin to restore its shape, coated with scented oils and wrapped in hundreds of metres of linen bandages. Between the layers, the priests slipped protective amulets, notably a scarab placed over the heart, engraved with a spell forbidding it to testify against its owner. For the wealthiest, a funerary mask covered the face of the mummy.

The Opening of the Mouth

On the day of the funeral, the mummy was carried in procession to the tomb. Before the entrance, a priest performed the rite of the Opening of the Mouth: by touching the mouth, eyes and ears of the mummy with sacred tools, he gave the deceased back the use of their senses, so that they could speak, see, hear, eat and breathe in the afterlife. The mummy was then laid in its sarcophagus, surrounded by figurines, food and everyday objects.

The journey through the afterlife

The deceased then set out on a long journey through the Duat, the world of the dead, full of gates guarded by demons, serpents and lakes of fire. To get past each obstacle, they had to know the right spell. These spells were gathered in a collection placed in the tomb, which the Egyptians called the “Book of Coming Forth by Day” and which we know as the Book of the Dead.

The judgement of the soul

At the end of the journey, the deceased was led into the Hall of the Two Truths, where Osiris sat, surrounded by forty-two judges. Before each of them, they had to declare that they had committed no wrong: “I have not stolen, I have not killed, I have not lied…” This list of denied sins is known as the “negative confession”.

Then came the decisive ordeal, the weighing of the heart, also called psychostasia. Anubis placed the heart of the deceased on one pan of a scale and, on the other, the feather of Maat, symbol of truth and justice. Thoth, the scribe of the gods, recorded the result. If the heart, weighed down by wrongdoing, tipped the scale, it was at once devoured by Amemet, a monster with the head of a crocodile, the forequarters of a lion and the hindquarters of a hippopotamus: the deceased was destroyed for ever.

If, on the contrary, the heart was as light as the feather, the deceased was declared “true of voice”. Horus took them by the hand and led them before the throne of Osiris, who welcomed them into his kingdom. Now an akh, the deceased could live for ever in the Field of Reeds, a paradise in the image of Egypt, where harvests were plentiful and the wheat grew taller than a man.
