The myth of the Distant Goddess tells of the flight of the Eye of Re, a lioness goddess gone off into the deserts of the south, and of her return to Egypt. It has no single version: it survives in fragments in the temples of the late period, from Philae to Dendera, and in a long story in Demotic, the Myth of the Eye of the Sun. Depending on the place, the goddess is called Tefnut, Hathor, Sekhmet or Bastet.

The Eye of Re

The Eye of Re is both a part of the sun god and an independent goddess: his daughter, his protector and the weapon of his wrath. In the days when Re ruled on earth, he once sent her far away. In her absence, he made himself another eye to replace her. When the goddess came back and found that another had taken her place, her fury was terrible. Wounded, she left Egypt.

The flight to Nubia

Changed into a lioness, the goddess fled into the deserts of Nubia, south of Egypt. There she lived as a wild beast, hunting, her eyes blazing with fire and her breath burning like the desert wind. Deprived of his Eye, Re was defenceless against his enemies, and Egypt itself was wasting away: without the goddess, the world was losing its balance.

Thoth and the fables

Re then sent Thoth to fetch her. The god of wisdom took the form of a small baboon so as to approach her without frightening her. Rather than force her, he talked to her: he described the beauties of Egypt and the offerings awaiting her in her temples, and told her animal fables to soothe her. In one of them, a lion spares a mouse which, later, saves him by gnawing through the ropes of a net: even the weakest have their place in the order of the world.

Little by little, the lioness’s anger subsided. In another version, it is Shu, or the hunter god Onuris, “he who brings back the distant one”, who goes to find her and brings her back before marrying her.

The return

The goddess agreed to come back. All along her journey up the Nile, she was greeted with songs, music and dances. At Philae, she bathed in the cool waters of the river, which put out her fury: the lioness became a gentle cat again, or the beautiful goddess Hathor. So that she would never leave again, Re placed her on his brow in the form of a rearing cobra, the uraeus: the Eye became the guardian of his crown.

The flood and the Festival of Drunkenness

The return of the goddess coincided with the arrival of the Nile flood, in the height of summer, when the waters coming from the south brought Egypt back to life. Every year this return was celebrated with a Festival of Drunkenness, notably at Dendera, in honour of Hathor: people drank, sang and danced there to soothe the goddess and make sure she would stay, benevolent, in Egypt.

This myth echoes that of the destruction of humankind: in both, the terrifying lioness goddess is finally calmed and transformed into a gentle, joyful deity. Through it, the Egyptians expressed the twofold nature of the sun, which gives life but can also kill, and the need to soothe it constantly through rites.
