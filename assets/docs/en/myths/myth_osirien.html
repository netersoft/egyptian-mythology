The birth of Nut's children

Re, the sun god, kept a close watch over the whole world as well as the Duat (the afterlife). One day he discovered that Nut was pregnant by Geb. Flying into a great rage, he forbade the goddess to give birth. Yet Nut had no way out: when her time came her children would be born, and Re would punish her. She tried in vain to hide, but the sun god saw everything and left nothing to chance. It was the god Thoth who came to her rescue, for he was in love with Nut himself and would have done anything for her. He devised a clever plan to outwit Re: a game of dice against the Moon. After a very long game, Thoth won and claimed his stake from the Moon: extra days in the year (the epagomenal days) during which Re could not watch what the gods were doing. That is how Nut was able to bring her children into the world without suffering Re's prying gaze.
On the first day she gave birth to Osiris, heir to Geb and therefore the next king of the Earth. Next came Horus the Elder, then, the following day, Seth. The daughters were born on the last two days: Isis and Nephthys. It is also possible that the five children did not all have the same father, for Nut had many suitors.

The duality of Osiris and Seth

Osiris, first son of Geb and Nut, has limbs of gold, hair of lapis lazuli (a blue semi-precious stone) and a crown of turquoise. He is immensely tall (4.5 metres), even for a god, and very strong. His intelligence is unrivalled, and he makes it his weapon, wielding words like no one else. So great is his splendour that all the gods accept him as their king after his father Geb. His influence brings prosperity and happiness to all people in the Two Lands and beyond their borders, and his presence is such that no enemy dares stand in his way. Osiris is a god who obtains whatever he wants through speech: he even persuades his sister and wife Isis to stay with him after his infidelity with their other sister, Nephthys, with whom he had a son, Anubis. Isis, too, is a being of great beauty: she fills the palace with dazzling light and is respected as much as her husband when he is away on his civilising missions, such as revealing agriculture and animal husbandry to humankind. Osiris's tragic fate marks the start of an unprecedented political crisis among the gods, which will end with the first pharaoh, Horus, ascending the throne of Egypt.

Seth is a very dark figure, full of hatred and violence. His birth was a terrible ordeal for his mother Nut: he burst out through her side, tearing it open to come out faster. In appearance, Seth is a small being, red like the desert sand, and he stands for barrenness. He had no children with his sister and wife Nephthys; so, to avoid revealing her infidelity with Osiris, she gave Anubis to Isis, who took him in as her faithful guide and right hand.

Osiris has no equal, and his brother Seth, his exact opposite, consumed by jealousy, resolves to plot against him and kill him.

Seth's trickery

Seth could not face Osiris directly: he had to use cunning, because this giant god was too powerful for anyone, his might so great that it came close to that of the demiurge himself, the sun god and head of the Ennead, Re. Seth invited Aso, the queen of Ethiopia, whom he had won over to his murderous cause, to a sumptuous banquet attended by, among others, the whole Ennead. In the middle of the hall stood an imposing, finely crafted chest covered in gold and precious stones. It shone so brightly that everyone wanted it. Seth then proposed a game with the chest as the prize: one by one the guests would lie down in it, and whoever fitted it would own it. Of course, the only one whose stature made him worthy of it was Osiris. Before he had time to get up again, seventy-two of Seth's henchmen rushed forward to shut him in, nailing the lid down with great nails and sealing it with molten lead. The servants of the treacherous brother carried the chest, with Osiris inside, to the Nile and threw it in. The reign of Osiris was over. Some say it had lasted 400 years, others 28, like a lunar cycle, for 28 human years are only 28 days for a god.

The consequences of a tragic death

When the death of Osiris became known, the whole world faltered: the elements ran wild and everything threatened to fall back into primordial chaos. Nature itself was stricken: the sun barely rose, the moon was slow to climb, the earth lay devastated. Humans wept, and so did the gods. But their tears brought forth life wherever they touched the dry ground, and in this way the blessings of Osiris lived on even after his death. Geb, in his grief, bled from the nose, and wherever the drops fell, resin-rich pines sprang up. From the tears of Shu and Tefnut came terebinth resin; from the tears of Re came bees, which would gather nectar from flowers and produce wax and honey. From the sweat of Re, exhausted by the disasters he was struggling to contain, sprouted the flax from which the linen of the priests' robes would be woven. Re's spittle and vomit turned into bitumen and papyrus. It is even said that Horus, not yet born, mourned his father and created frankincense by letting his tears fall to the ground.

Isis' first quest

Isis, meanwhile, was in mourning. Her husband's death was a terrible blow that grieved her deeply, but she had no intention of standing idle. She set out across the world in search of Osiris's body so as to give him the honours due to the dead. Helped by her sister Nephthys, Isis went looking for her late husband. On her way she met children who told her they had seen the chest in which Osiris lay. Following their directions, Isis traced the river down to its mouth. Listening only to her heart, and guided by the wind that had driven the chest through the waters, she crossed the marshes of the delta and reached the sea. Following the coast, as if guided by her late husband, she came to the city of Byblos, where the chest of Osiris had run aground against a tree. Touched by the divine nature of the coffin, this tamarisk had grown until it enclosed it completely. Marvelling at this immense tree, the king of Byblos had it cut down and made into a pillar for his palace. Isis went to the king's palace to find her husband. Passing herself off as an ordinary woman, she became nurse to the queen's son and, as if he were her own child, gave him her finger to suckle and each night burned away everything mortal in him. But the queen came upon this ritual and interrupted it, and the goddess then revealed herself in all her divine splendour. She was given the chest in which her husband lay, and left the king the pillar, which became an object of veneration.
Taking the form of a bird carrying an ankh (the cross of life), and joined by her sister in the same form, Isis opened Osiris's coffin. Together they beat their wings over the lifeless body of the greatest of the gods, to send air into his nostrils and bring him back to life. But it was not enough. Isis whispered these words to her late husband: "Osiris, see, your sister Isis has come, your wife, opening herself to your love. Place her upon your phallus, so that what comes forth from you may be in her." Osiris was partly revived, and she was able to unite with him one last time and receive his seed, which made her fertile.

Isis was now pregnant, but the world was shaken: this union was against nature and should never have happened, and it upset the laws of nature. It was so cataclysmic that the sky was torn open and lightning lashed the night; even the gods were afraid. Isis was with child, and Horus was to be born of a dead father and reign over the land of Egypt. The birth of Horus had to be kept secret to avoid Seth's revenge, but one night Seth found Osiris's chest open and flew into a terrible rage. He cut Osiris's body into fourteen pieces and scattered them all over Egypt.

Isis' second quest and the first mummy

Learning of her treacherous brother's crime, Isis set out on a new quest: to gather the scattered pieces of her husband. Wherever she found one, she raised a temple in Osiris's honour. She was not alone in this quest: Nephthys was with her too, and it is even said that Horus, still a child, helped recover his father's remains. Isis is said to have found every part of her late husband's body except, perhaps, his phallus, which Seth had thrown to the fish. Anubis took charge of the mummification and wrapped the pieces of Osiris in a papyrus covering called the "nebris". The jackal-headed god carefully dried the body, softened it with ointments and swathed it in fine linen. It was the first mummy. The bodily fluids were collected in the senu vessel.

Isis spoke these words to her husband: "Osiris, live, and spread life around you. And you, sap of this shining body, spring forth, flow, fill the canals, become a river, quench our thirst." Then fluids were seen flowing from his body, sowing the fields, swelling the waters of the Nile and making the grain sprout. Having reigned over the world of the living, Osiris departed to reign over the world of the dead. When he arrived there, he received the sceptre, the flail and the shepherd's crook from the hands of Thoth, and became the ruler of the world of the dead, the "land of silence".
Osiris's body was buried in a sacred place, forbidden to humans, which only Isis visited, by boat. Offerings, however, were always made in honour and memory of the one who had brought peace and prosperity to the Two Lands. The world over which Osiris now reigned was seldom visited by the gods; only Thoth and Anubis acted as go-betweens. Though absent from the earth, Osiris remained present in the shining form of the constellation of Orion.

Horus' hidden childhood

Isis, pregnant with Osiris' last breath, knew that Seth would never let the rightful heir to the throne live. She took refuge in the marshes of Chemmis, deep in the delta, where papyrus and reeds formed a natural barrier against her brother's watchful eye. It was there, hidden from the world, that she gave birth to Horus — not to be confused with his uncle Horus the Elder, one of Nut's five children. This new Horus grew up far from the other gods, nursed by his mother and protected day and night by her magic, while Seth ruled Egypt from the throne he had stolen from his brother.

Isis and the seven scorpions

The young god's childhood was not a peaceful one, however. One day, as Isis fled through the delta with her son, seven scorpions joined her retinue to protect her: Tefen, Befen, Mestet, Mestetef, Petet, Thetet, and Matet. As evening fell, they reached a village where a wealthy woman, frightened by this procession of scorpions, slammed her door in their faces. A poor peasant woman named Taha, however, offered Isis and the child shelter.
Enraged by the wealthy woman's hostile welcome, the seven scorpions pooled their venom into the stinger of one of their number, Tefen, who slipped into the house during the night and stung the woman's young son. The child was seized with convulsions, and no one came to his weeping mother's aid, so widely despised was she for her hard-heartedness. Moved nonetheless by her cries, Isis took pity on the innocent child: she laid her hands upon him and spoke the magic formula naming each of the seven scorpions in turn, commanding their venom to leave his body. The child was saved, and the wealthy woman, ashamed of her conduct, gave all her possessions to poor Taha, who alone had known how to welcome the goddess.
This episode endured in Egyptian memory as proof of Isis' healing power: the formula she spoke that day was copied for centuries onto magical stelae, such as the famous Metternich Stela, consulted by the ancient Egyptians to treat scorpion stings.

The trials of the divine tribunal

Once grown, Horus appeared before the assembled Ennead to claim his father's throne. Seth objected at once: only he, he argued, had the strength to protect Egypt and to fend off Apophis every night aboard Re's barque. The tribunal of the gods, presided over by Re himself, hesitated for eighty years without reaching a verdict, favouring first one, then the other.
The two rivals then clashed directly, through cunning as much as through strength. Turned into hippopotamuses, they plunged into the Nile for a duel that Isis, fearing for her son's life, interrupted with an ill-fated harpoon that wounded Horus before she drew it back out, ashamed. Seth, furious at her interference, tore out his nephew's eyes and buried them; Hathor found them and healed them with gazelle's milk, restoring Horus' sight and, with it, the Eye of Horus — a symbol of protection and wholeness still found on amulets and sarcophagi today.
One final trial was arranged: a race of stone boats. Horus tricked his rival by secretly swapping his own boat for one of wood coated in plaster, which floated, while Seth's, carved from the true stone of a mountain, sank at once.

The final judgement and Horus' coronation

Weary of these endless quarrels, the tribunal was still on the verge of granting the throne to Seth when Osiris, from the kingdom of the dead, sent the gods a threatening letter: if Horus, his rightful son, was not crowned, he would send his messengers — the baleful spirits of the underworld — to demand justice among the living. Faced with this threat, Re and the Ennead finally reached their verdict: Horus was proclaimed the rightful king of the Two Lands, heir to his father.
Seth, however, was not destroyed: he was taken aboard Re's solar barque, where his brute strength was now put to use fending off Apophis, the serpent of chaos, every night — finding his place at last in the order of the world he had once tried to break. Horus, now crowned, became the model for every pharaoh who would reign over Egypt: each living king would henceforth be his earthly incarnation, while Osiris, from the afterlife, watched over the dead.
