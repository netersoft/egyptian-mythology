On the island of Sehel, near Aswan, a large granite boulder bears a long inscription topped by a scene: a king presents offerings to the ram god Khnum and to the goddesses Satet and Anuket. This is the Famine Stela. It was carved in the Ptolemaic period, around the 2nd century BC, but it tells of an event set more than two thousand years earlier, in the reign of King Djoser, the builder of the first pyramid.

Seven years of misery

The text makes King Djoser speak in person. For seven years the Nile flood had not come. Grain was scarce, the gardens were parched, people robbed their neighbours, children cried and the old, heartbroken, sat on the ground. The temples themselves were closed and the gods no longer received offerings.

In despair, the king turned to his wise adviser, Imhotep, the priest and architect who had built the Step Pyramid at Saqqara for him. He asked him where the Nile was born and which god ruled over the flood.

Imhotep’s answer

Imhotep went off to consult the sacred books kept in the temple archives. On his return, he revealed to the king the secret of the flood: the Nile gushed out of two caverns beneath the island of Elephantine, on the southern border of Egypt. The lord of these springs was Khnum, the ram-headed god. It was he who guarded the gates of the waters and decided to open them each year.

The king’s dream

That night, Khnum appeared to Djoser in a dream. The god presented himself as the one who had shaped humankind and who held the sources of the river in his hand. He reproached the king for neglecting his temple, then promised: “I will make the Nile rise for you. There will be no more years when the flood fails. Plants will grow and bend under their fruit, and the famine will come to an end.”

The donation

On waking, the grateful king issued a decree in favour of Khnum. He gave the temple of Elephantine all the lands stretching on either side of the river, southwards as far as Nubia, together with a share of all the harvests, livestock, fish and goods that passed through them. The decree was to be carved on a stela, so that no one could be unaware of it.

A pious forgery

Historians believe that this decree was never issued by Djoser. In the Ptolemaic period, the priests of Khnum at Elephantine were competing with those of Isis, on the neighbouring island of Philae, for the revenues of the region. By attributing their rights to a venerable king of the Old Kingdom, they gave them the authority of age. The stela nonetheless remains a precious witness to the Egyptians’ dread of low floods, and to the memory of Imhotep, who over the centuries became a deified sage, patron of scribes and physicians.
