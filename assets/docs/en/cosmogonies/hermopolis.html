Hermopolis, in Middle Egypt, was called Khemenu in Egyptian, the “city of the Eight”. Capital of the fifteenth nome (province) of Upper Egypt, it owes this name to the group of eight deities at the heart of its cosmogony: the Ogdoad. Its priests claimed it was the oldest of all creations, older even than that of Heliopolis, although the texts that describe it are mostly late.

Here, the world is not born of a single creator. Before anything else there was a formless chaos, which the theologians of Hermopolis described through four couples of gods, each embodying one of its qualities:

  • Nun and Naunet, the primordial waters;
  • Heh and Hauhet, boundless space;
  • Kek and Kauket, darkness;
  • Amun and Amaunet, what is hidden, invisible.

The gods of the Ogdoad were shown with the heads of frogs, the goddesses with the heads of serpents: animals that live in water and mud, where life seemed to arise of its own accord after the Nile flood.

The cosmic egg

The eight deities acted together. On a mound that rose from the waters, the “Isle of Flame”, they gave birth to the sun, which climbed into the sky and scattered the darkness. Their work done, they withdrew into the underworld, where they still make the Nile flow and the sun rise every morning.

Several versions tell how the sun appeared. In one, it came out of a cosmic egg laid on the primordial mound. The egg was said to have been laid by a celestial goose, the “Great Cackler”, whose cry was the first sound to break the silence of chaos; later, this role was given to the ibis of Thoth. The priests of Hermopolis even showed pilgrims fragments of the shell, kept in their temple.

The primordial lotus

According to another version, a lotus flower rose from the waters of the Nun. As it opened, it revealed a divine child sitting in its corolla: the young sun, sometimes identified with Re, sometimes with the god Nefertem. In one variant, the flower released a scarab, Khepri, image of the sun that renews itself, which then turned into a child. The blue lotus, which opens in the morning and closes in the evening, was the perfect symbol of this sun reborn every day.

Thoth, lord of Hermopolis

Hermopolis was above all the city of Thoth, god of wisdom, writing and the moon. Over time, his priests gave him a growing place in Creation: Thoth became the one who had set the Ogdoad in order, or even the one who had brought it into being by his word. The city had vast cemeteries of sacred animals, notably at Tuna el-Gebel, where millions of mummified ibises and baboons, the two animals of Thoth, have been found.

The city’s prestige lasted into the Graeco-Roman period. The Greeks, who saw in Thoth their god Hermes, messenger of the gods and patron of letters, gave it the name Hermopolis, the “city of Hermes”.
