This cosmogony comes from Hermopolis, Khemenu in Egyptian (meaning "city of the eight", after the Ogdoad), capital of the 15th nome. Several versions of it exist; this is the main one. Its patron god is Thoth (god of wisdom, knowledge and the moon, and master of writing). Thoth undertook the creation of the world with the help of eight creator gods: four male gods, each with a goddess as his female counterpart. The males were given the heads of frogs and the females the heads of snakes.

These eight gods, who form the Ogdoad, are Nun and Naunet (the watery element), Heh and Hehet (infinity), Kek and Keket (darkness), and Amun and Amaunet (the mysterious, "hidden" element). They brooded over a strange egg laid by Thoth on the primordial mound that had just risen out of the Nun (probably a metaphor for the flooding Nile, from which only mounds emerge), until the sun hatched from it and rose into the sky.

This cosmogony is said to be the oldest in Egypt, although documents about it are very rare before the Middle Kingdom. As at Heliopolis, before Creation there was something inert that already held every element needed for a demiurge to emerge.

The lotus and the cosmic egg

According to one variant of this myth, it was not only an egg but also a lotus flower that emerged from the waters of the Nun on the primordial mound. As it opened, it revealed either a divine child seated on its calyx, foreshadowing the god Nefertem, or the solar scarab Khepri, an image of the reborn sun. Another tradition attributes the laying of the cosmic egg not to Thoth but to a wild goose nicknamed "the Great Cackler," whose cry is said to have broken the silence of the primordial chaos even before the birth of light.

An enduring cult

Although the Hermopolitan cosmogony gradually lost ground to that of Heliopolis, Hermopolis remained a major religious centre devoted to Thoth: the city held vast necropolises dedicated to ibises and baboons, the god's two sacred animals, of which millions of mummies have been found. The site's intellectual prestige endured into the Greco-Roman period, when the Greeks identified the city with Hermes and renamed it "Hermopolis," the city of Hermes, echoing the shared role of divine messenger and scribe that Thoth and Hermes both held.
