People have always sought to explain the origin of the world: how Creation came about and gave birth to the earth, the water, the plants, the animals and human beings. The ancient Egyptians were no exception.

A cosmogony is a mythical account of the creation of the world (from the Greek kosmos, “world”, and gonos, “begetting”). Egypt did not have one, but several: each great religious centre developed its own, in which the god of the city plays the part of the demiurge, the creator of the universe (from the Greek dēmiourgos, “craftsman”).

The four main cosmogonies are those of Heliopolis, Hermopolis, Memphis and Thebes. Far from excluding one another, they coexisted and borrowed from each other: all of them make the world rise from a primordial ocean, the Nun, and from a mound of earth that emerged from the waters. They differ mainly in who the creator is and how he acts: by begetting himself, by hatching a cosmic egg, through thought and speech, or by absorbing the creations of his rivals.

The most widespread is that of Heliopolis. Its creator is a solar demiurge, Atum, often identified with Re, and it sets out a divine genealogy that leads down to Horus, the model for every pharaoh.
