Thebes, Waset in Egyptian, was at first only a small town in Upper Egypt. It became the capital of the country in the Middle Kingdom and again in the New Kingdom, and its god, Amun, rose with it to the top of the pantheon. Coming late into religious history, Thebes could not ignore the older cosmogonies: its theologians wove all of them into their own account, showing that Amun stood at their origin.

Amun, “the hidden one”, is an invisible creator, present everywhere but seen by no one. He formed himself at the beginning, without father or mother, and all the other gods are merely his manifestations. A hymn of the period sums it up: “Three are all the gods: Amun, Re and Ptah.” Amun is the hidden name, Re the visible face, and Ptah the body.

Kematef, Irta and the Ogdoad

According to late texts, Amun appeared at the dawn of the world in the form of a serpent, Kematef, “he who has completed his time”, rising from the Nun on the very site of Thebes. Kematef begot another serpent, Irta, “he who made the earth”. Irta in turn created the eight deities of the Ogdoad, taken over from the cosmogony of Hermopolis.

The Ogdoad then set out to create the world: it went to Memphis, where it brought Ptah into being, then to Heliopolis, where it brought forth Atum. Its work done, it returned to Thebes to die. The eight gods have rested ever since at Djeme, on the west bank, where Amun regularly came to worship them.

This account is a clever theological feat: it makes Amun the ancestor of the Ogdoad of Hermopolis, of Ptah of Memphis and of Atum of Heliopolis, and therefore the source of all creations. Other texts go as far as making Amun himself the goose that laid the cosmic egg.

The Theban triad

At Thebes, Amun formed a divine family with his wife Mut, a mother goddess shown as a vulture or as a woman wearing the double crown, and their son Khonsu, god of the moon. Every year, during the Opet festival, the statue of Amun left the temple of Karnak and travelled upriver by boat to the temple of Luxor, accompanied by those of Mut and Khonsu, amid songs and offerings: there the god renewed his power and that of the king.

The height of Amun’s power

In the New Kingdom, Amun, merged with the sun god under the name Amun-Re, became the god of the dynasty, “king of the gods”. Victorious pharaohs credited him with their conquests and showered him with riches: enlarged over nearly two thousand years, the temple of Karnak became the largest religious complex ever built in Egypt.

The priesthood of Amun gathered so much wealth and influence that it came to rival the king. The pharaoh Akhenaten tried to break this power by imposing the cult of the Aten alone, but Amun was restored in the very next generation. At the end of the New Kingdom, the high priests of Amun even ruled all of Upper Egypt from Thebes.
