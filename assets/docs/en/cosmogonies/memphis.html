The story of the Memphite myth is a remarkable one. In 1805, the British Museum received into its Egyptian collection a rectangular stone, damaged because an Egyptian farmer had used it as a millstone. It bears 63 columns of finely carved text, but at the time no one could yet read hieroglyphs. The stone in fact carries a religious text of exceptional importance: the Memphite Theology, which tells the creation of the world according to the cosmogony of Memphis, the ancient capital south of modern Cairo. The monument dates from the 25th dynasty, more precisely from the reign of Shabaka. As in the other myths, the Word lies at the heart of everything, but here speech brings into being whatever it expresses.

The demiurge of Memphis is neither Atum nor Thoth, but Ptah ("sculptor of the earth", "the one who opens"). "Ptah conceives the world through the thought of his heart and gives it life through the magic of his Word." Some documents also explain that Ptah conceives through Thought (represented by Horus) and creates through Speech (represented by Thoth).

The creative power of the Word

In this theology, every element of the world exists first as a thought in Ptah's heart before taking on real form once it is spoken aloud. This power of the creative Word, able to instantly bring into being whatever it names, sets Ptah above even the other demiurges: he need not separate sky from earth or shape matter by hand — he need only conceive, then speak. Having created the other gods, Atum included, Ptah went on to shape cities and temples and to establish the first funerary rites.

Memphis, religious capital

Seat of royal power from the first dynasty onward, Memphis had to build a theology able to rival the older one of Heliopolis. By placing Ptah at the summit of creation, its priests made their city the original stage of the world. The cult of Ptah went hand in hand with that of the Apis bull, regarded as his living incarnation on earth, and Ptah eventually merged with Sokar and Osiris into the composite form Ptah-Sokar-Osiris, a funerary deity worshipped throughout Egypt into the late period.
