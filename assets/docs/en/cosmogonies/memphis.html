Memphis, at the head of the Delta, some twenty kilometres south of present-day Cairo, was the capital of Egypt from the first dynasties. Its great god was Ptah, patron of craftsmen, and the name of his temple, Hut-ka-Ptah, “the estate of the ka of Ptah”, became Aiguptos in Greek: this is where the word “Egypt” comes from.

As the political capital, Memphis also wanted to be the country’s religious capital. Its priests developed a theology able to rival the older and more prestigious one of Heliopolis: they did not deny the existence of Atum and the Ennead, but made Ptah the one who had created them.

The Shabaka Stone

This theology has come down to us thanks to a remarkable monument. Around 710 BC, the pharaoh Shabaka, of the 25th Dynasty, found in the temple of Ptah an old papyrus eaten by worms. To save the text, he had it carved on a slab of black granite. Centuries later, the stone was used as a millstone, and the grinding wore away much of its centre.

Given to the British Museum in 1805, when no one could yet read hieroglyphs, the Shabaka Stone only revealed its content in the 20th century. It holds one of the most remarkable texts of Egyptian thought.

The creative power of speech

In Memphite theology, Ptah creates neither by coupling with himself, like Atum, nor by hatching an egg, like the Ogdoad: he creates through the mind. Everything that exists was first a thought conceived in his heart, which the Egyptians held to be the seat of intelligence, then a word spoken by his tongue. The moment Ptah named a thing, it existed.

The text states that the heart of Ptah is Horus and his tongue is Thoth. The gods of the Ennead themselves, Atum among them, are described as “the teeth and lips” of his mouth, the instruments of his speech. Having created the gods, Ptah founded the cities and provinces, installed the gods in their shrines, established their offerings and gave shape to their statues.

Ptah was also identified with Tatenen, “the risen land”, the primordial mound that came out of the waters. Creator through thought and raw material of the world, he thus unites the two faces of the demiurge.

The Memphite triad and its cults

At Memphis, Ptah formed a divine family with the lioness goddess Sekhmet, his wife, and their son Nefertem, god of the lotus. His cult went hand in hand with that of the Apis bull, regarded as his living incarnation: when it died, the animal was mummified and buried with great ceremony in the underground galleries of the Serapeum at Saqqara, before a new bull, recognisable by precise markings on its coat, took its place.

Ptah finally merged with two other gods of the region, Sokar, god of the necropolis, and Osiris, ruler of the dead, to form Ptah-Sokar-Osiris. This single deity brought together creation, death and rebirth, and was worshipped throughout Egypt until the latest periods.
