Heliopolis, the Greeks’ “city of the sun”, was called Iunu in Egyptian. Lying at the gates of present-day Cairo, it was from the Old Kingdom onwards the great centre of the sun cult, and its priests shaped the most widespread creation story in Egypt.

In the beginning there was only a boundless ocean, inert and plunged in darkness: the Nun. From these waters rose Atum, whose name means both “the Complete One” and “the One who does not yet exist”: he held within himself everything that was to be. Atum created himself by becoming aware of his own existence. He then raised a mound of earth out of the waters, the Benben, so that he could stand on it. Some texts say he appeared there in the form of the Bennu, a heron-like bird that the Greeks later likened to the phoenix.

Alone, without a companion, Atum drew a first divine couple out of himself. Depending on the version, he coupled with his own hand, or he spat and sneezed: from his sneeze came Shu, god of air and light, and from his spittle Tefnut, goddess of moisture. Brother and sister, they were also husband and wife, and they form the first couple of Creation.

With them came Maat, the order of the world. More than a goddess, Maat is the principle that holds the universe together: justice, truth, the regular course of the sun and the seasons. She is opposed to isfet, disorder, which constantly threatens to drag the world back into the chaos of the beginning.

The birth of sky and earth

From the union of Shu and Tefnut were born Geb, the earth, and Nut, the sky. The two were so tightly entwined that nothing could exist between them. At Atum’s command, Shu slipped between his children and lifted Nut up at arm’s length, while Geb remained lying on the ground. So the space opened up where air circulates, where light shines and where life can grow.

Arched above the earth, Nut now touches her husband only with the tips of her fingers and toes. The sun travels across her body by day; every evening she swallows it, and every morning she gives birth to it anew. The Nun was pushed back beyond the limits of this world, which it still surrounds like a silent threat.

The birth of humankind

Humans, too, owe their existence to the demiurge. One story tells that Shu and Tefnut, still children, had lost their way in the darkness of the Nun. Atum sent his Eye to look for them. When he saw them come back, he wept for joy, and his tears gave birth to the first human beings.

The Coffin Texts, funerary texts of the Middle Kingdom (around 2000–1700 BC), have the creator say: “Humans are the tears of my eye.” The story rests on a play on words: in Egyptian, “tears” is remyt and “humans” remetch.

The children of Nut

The Egyptian calendar had twelve months of thirty days, making 360 days. According to a legend told by Plutarch, Re, fearing he would be overthrown, had forbidden Nut to give birth on any day of the year. Thoth, the god of wisdom, came to her aid: he played dice with the Moon and won from her a small share of her light, enough to make five extra days, set outside the year. During these days, Nut was at last able to bring her children into the world.

These five days are called “epagomenal”, from the Greek epagomenai hēmerai, the “added days”. On each of them a god was born: Osiris on the first, Horus the Elder on the second (not to be confused with Horus, the son of Isis and Osiris), Seth on the third, Isis on the fourth and Nephthys on the fifth. The Egyptians held these days to be dangerous, especially the third, the day of Seth’s birth.

The Ennead

The theologians of Heliopolis gathered these gods into a group of nine, the Ennead, which brings together the forces needed for an ordered world to exist: the demiurge Atum, his children Shu and Tefnut, his grandchildren Geb and Nut, then their children, except for Horus the Elder, namely the two couples formed by Osiris and Isis, Seth and Nephthys. Atum, often merged with Re under the name Re-Atum, stands at the head of this divine family.

The forces of chaos

Creation did not make the Nun disappear: it only pushed it back. Everything that was not created goes on existing at its edges and threatens the established order. The most fearsome of these forces is Apophis, a gigantic serpent born of the primordial waters. Every evening, when the sun leaves the daytime sky, its bark sets out for twelve hours into the world of night, and Apophis tries to devour it. Like Atum, Apophis was never created: he therefore cannot be destroyed, only driven back, night after night, so that the sun can be reborn at dawn.
