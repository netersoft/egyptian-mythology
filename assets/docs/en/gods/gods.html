Egyptian mythology is both fascinating and complex, and it has several distinctive features:
  • the many faces of the gods, since most deities could be represented in several ways. The animal form is probably the oldest, but the purely human form was added very early on; the composite form, combining a human body with an animal head or vice versa, came later;
  • the importance given to symbols, through animals;
  • the local roots of most gods, and the cycles that run through the myths.
The gods were ever present in the daily life of the Egyptian people, who looked to them for support and protection against the forces of evil.
Here are the main gods of the Egyptian pantheon:

Amemet

The goddess Amemet is depicted with the hindquarters of a hippopotamus, the forequarters of a lion and the head of a crocodile. At the weighing of hearts in the afterlife, she "devoured" the hearts of those found guilty.

Amemet never had a true cult or a temple of her own: feared rather than worshipped, she exists only within the context of the judgment of the soul, described notably in the Book of the Dead. Her other name, "the Great of Death," recalls that she represents not bodily death but the final annihilation of a being whose heart, heavier than Maat's feather, reveals an unjust life.

Amun

Chief of the gods under the New Kingdom, Amun took the form of a man wearing a headdress adorned with two tall plumes, or else that of a ram or a goose. With his wife Mut and their son Khonsu, he formed the Theban triad, the sacred family of Thebes. Amun came to acquire considerable importance, yet never became a state god. He was associated with the god Re and worshipped in the form of Amun-Re.

Amun-Re

Amun-Re is one of the forms of the sun god and is sometimes depicted as a sphinx or as a human figure with a falcon's head. The solar disc is his emblem. The name "Amun" means "the hidden one", or the hidden nature of the divine, while "Re" or "Ra" means "the sun", or the divinity revealed through the sun's power. The god Amun-Re unites these two ideas: an invisible power that is ever present, and the dazzling light of the divine force that sustains life. To trace the origins of Amun-Re, we must go back to the Old Kingdom and to Heliopolis, where Re first appeared as the chief manifestation of the sun god. Re is shown with the head of a falcon crowned by the solar disc as he crosses the sky, and with the head of a ram as he completes his night journey through the underworld. This local god rose to become a national god, and solar temples were built throughout the country. Under the 4th dynasty, the pharaohs began to regard themselves as incarnations of this god. Later, during the Middle Kingdom, when Amun became the principal god, Re was merged with him as Amun-Re.

Anubis

Anubis was a jackal-headed god who presided over embalming and accompanied dead kings into the afterlife. When the kings came before Osiris to be judged, Anubis placed their heart on one pan of a scale and a feather (representing Maat) on the other. The god Thoth recorded the result, on which the king's right to enter the afterlife depended. Anubis is the son of Osiris and Nephthys.

Apophis

Apophis (or Apep, "he who was spat out") is the giant serpent of primordial chaos, born from the Nun even before the ordered world came into being. Unlike the other gods, he was never created and can therefore never be truly destroyed: every night, he lies in wait for Re's solar barque at a critical point of its journey through the Duat, seeking to swallow the sun and plunge the universe back into primeval chaos.

Apophis had no temple or cult of his own — quite the opposite, he embodied everything that Egyptian religion sought to hold at bay. A daily ritual, the "Book of Overthrowing Apophis," was recited by priests in temples across the land: a wax effigy of the serpent was fashioned, spat upon, trampled, slashed, and burned, to magically help Re defeat his enemy each night. His defeat, however, is never final: Apophis is endlessly reborn, a reminder that the struggle between order (Maat) and chaos knows no end.

Aten

A primordial cosmic god, Aten is the sun god as creator, the substance from which all creation is drawn. He is the lord of the universe. In human form, he represents the king of Egypt, wearing the double crown of Egypt.

Though Aten had been worshipped since the Old Kingdom as a simple manifestation of the solar disc, it was the pharaoh Akhenaten who, in the 14th century BCE, made him the sole god of his kingdom, closing the temples of the other deities and founding a new capital, Akhetaten (modern Amarna), entirely dedicated to his worship. This episode, known as the Amarna heresy, remains the most significant attempt at monotheism in ancient Egypt; Aten's cult was abolished shortly after Akhenaten's death and the traditional polytheistic order restored.

Bastet

A cat goddess, Bastet embodies the most protective aspects of motherhood, in contrast to the fierce, lion-headed Sekhmet. Shown with the body of a woman and the head of a cat, she often carries a sistrum.

Her main cult centre was Bubastis, in the Nile delta, where an immense temple was dedicated to her and where, each year, a festival drew thousands of pilgrims in a joyous celebration described by Herodotus. Daughter of Re, she watched over the home and fertility; cats, considered sacred, were raised near her sanctuaries and then mummified and offered as votive gifts by the thousand.

Bes

This dwarf god, whose thick-lipped face looks like a grotesque mask, often carries musical instruments, knives or the hieroglyph for protection. Despite his appearance, he is the protector of the family and is associated with sexuality and childbirth.

Unlike most Egyptian gods, Bes never had a great temple: his cult was above all domestic, popular, and protective. His image adorned beds, headrests, and everyday objects, particularly in birthing rooms, where his grimaces and tambourine were believed to drive away the malevolent spirits threatening mother and newborn.

Shu & Tefnut

Shu is a male god paired with his sister, Tefnut. Together they personify two fundamental principles of human existence: Shu stands for dry air and the force of preservation, while Tefnut embodies the moist, corrosive air that brings change and so gives rise to the notion of time. Shu and Tefnut are the children of Re (or of Atum, a form of the sun god), a primordial cosmic god who brought forth the elements of the universe.

Geb & Nut

These two gods are each other's counterpart. Geb, an earth god who represents the dry land, is the husband of Nut, a sky goddess who embodies the heavens, the mirror image of the sea. Nut is shown as a woman bearing the hieroglyph of her name on her head, or as a woman whose arched body is covered with stars. They are the children of Shu and Tefnut.

Hapi

Hapi, "he who brings abundance", brought about the yearly flooding of the Nile, which fertilised the land and made it possible to farm it. He was shown as a man with plants in his hair, holding an offering table laden with the fruits of the earth.

Although he never had a temple in his own name, Hapi received hymns and ritual offerings near the first cataract, close to Aswan, where the flood was believed to originate. His androgynous form, with generous curves and a woman's breasts, symbolised the river's nourishing abundance; he was sometimes depicted in pairs, one bearing the plants of the South and the other those of the North, embodying the union of the Two Lands through the waters of the Nile.

Hathor

This goddess can take three forms: a woman holding a menat necklace and wearing a crown of cow's horns enclosing the solar disc; a woman with a cow's ears; or a cow. She is the daughter of Re and the wife of Horus, the celestial falcon god. She is also regarded as the mother of every reigning king (like the goddess Isis). Hathor's nature is twofold: in her vengeful aspect she took on the lioness form of the goddess Sekhmet and tried to destroy humankind after the revolt told in the creation myth; in her cow form she is associated with sexuality, joy and music.

Horus

Horus, the falcon-headed god, holds a prominent place in Egypt. He is the son of Osiris and Isis, the divine child of the sacred triad — not to be confused with his uncle Horus the Elder, one of Nut's five children, born before him. He is one of the many gods associated with the falcon, and his name means "he who is above" as well as "he who is far away". The falcon was worshipped from the earliest times as a cosmic deity: its body represented the sky, and its eyes the sun and the moon. Horus is depicted as a falcon wearing a crown with a cobra, or the double crown of Egypt. The cobra (uraeus), worn on the brow by gods and pharaohs alone, symbolises light and kingship; its role is to protect its wearer from danger. When Horus was a child, his father was murdered by his uncle Seth. To keep her son safe, Isis hid him in the marshes of the Nile, where she shielded him from venomous snakes, scorpions, crocodiles and wild animals. As he grew up, he learned to defend himself and became strong enough to fight Seth and claim his rightful inheritance, the throne of Egypt. Horus is therefore associated with kingship and personifies divine and royal power. The kings believed they were descended from Horus, who was regarded as the first divine king of Egypt.

Isis

Isis embodies the power of love to overcome death. She brought her brother and husband, Osiris, back to life and saved her son Horus from certain death. She is shown with the hieroglyph for "throne" on her head; from the New Kingdom onward, she sometimes wore a solar disc between cow's horns. She is also often depicted mourning her husband or nursing her son.

Her great cult centre stood at Philae, at Egypt's southernmost tip, where her temple remained active until the 6th century CE, one of the last functioning pagan sanctuaries of antiquity. Regarded as the most powerful magician in the pantheon — she was the one who wrested his secret name from Re — she bore the epithet "great of magic." Her cult spread far beyond Egypt: the mysteries of Isis reached across the Greco-Roman world, as far as Rome itself, where she became one of the Empire's most widely worshipped foreign deities.

Khnum

A ram-headed god with horizontal horns, Khnum is often shown shaping human beings on his potter's wheel. He emerged from two caverns of the underworld in the ocean of the Nun. He was the god of the First Cataract of the Nile, in Upper Egypt, and ensured fertility by sending half of the river's waters south and the other half north.

His main sanctuary stood at Elephantine, on Egypt's southern border, where he formed a triad with the goddesses Satet and Anuket. Regarded as the "divine potter," he was said to shape on his wheel not only every human body but also its spiritual double, the ka, before placing them in the mother's womb — making him one of the few creator gods whose myth survived, in a local form, into the Greco-Roman period at the temple of Esna.

Khonsu

Son of the Theban couple Amun and Mut, Khonsu is a moon god often shown with a falcon's head crowned by a crescent moon and a lunar disc. He also often appears as a young man with a sidelock of hair falling beside his face, his head crowned by a crescent moon and a lunar disc.

His temple, still standing today, rises at the heart of the great Karnak complex in Thebes, where he formed the Theban triad with his parents Amun and Mut. A god of time and the reckoning of months, he was also invoked as a healer: legend tells of a statue of Khonsu sent as far as Syria to free a foreign princess from an evil spirit.

Maat

Maat is the goddess of truth and justice. She is depicted as a woman wearing a headband adorned with an ostrich feather.

Daughter of Re, Maat embodies far more than a goddess: she is the very principle of cosmic order, truth, and justice upon which the balance of the world rests. Every pharaoh had to "live by Maat" to guarantee the harmony of his kingdom, and it was her feather that, during the judgment of the soul, was placed against the deceased's heart on the scales — only a heart as light as her feather could pass into the afterlife.

Min

Shown in human or mummiform shape, Min is depicted with an erect phallus and a crown topped by two plumes. In his raised right hand he holds a flail. He is the protector of fertility and of travellers in the desert.

Worshipped mainly at Akhmim and Coptos, gateways to the eastern desert and the trade routes to the Red Sea, Min was celebrated each year in a great agricultural festival marking the start of the harvest, in which the pharaoh took part in person to symbolically renew his royal fertility. He was later merged with Amun as Amun-Min-Kamutef, "the bull of his mother," a god capable of regenerating himself.

Mut

Wife of the Theban god Amun, Mut was first depicted as a vulture and later as a woman. With her husband Amun and their son Khonsu, she formed the Theban triad, the sacred family of Thebes.

Her main sanctuary, south of the great temple of Amun at Karnak, was surrounded by a crescent-shaped sacred lake, the Isheru. Symbolic adoptive mother of every pharaoh, Mut could also take on a vengeful aspect close to that of Sekhmet, a reminder that Egypt's protective goddesses often carried within them this dual nature, both benevolent and fearsome.

Nefertem

Nefertem is the god of the blue lotus flower and of perfume, usually depicted as a young man wearing a full-blown lotus blossom on his head, sometimes topped with two tall plumes. According to Memphite theology, he is the son of Ptah and Sekhmet, completing the Memphite triad; his name is thought to mean "he who is beautiful."

At the dawn of the world, a giant lotus flower is said to have emerged from the primordial waters of the Nun and, as it opened, released the fragrance of Nefertem, closely linking the god to the breath of life and the birth of the sun. Each morning, the pharaoh symbolically inhaled this fragrance during royal rituals, believed to grant youth and renewal, before the god was later given a leonine form, taking on a warrior aspect close to that of his mother Sekhmet.

Neith

A goddess associated with war and weaving, Neith is, together with Isis, Nephthys and Selkis, one of the key figures of the funerary cult. She is usually shown wearing the red crown of Lower Egypt.

One of the oldest deities of the Egyptian pantheon, Neith was worshipped as early as the Predynastic period and became the great goddess of Sais, capital of the 26th Dynasty. Some texts even make her a creator goddess, self-born before all other gods and mother of Re. In funerary worship, she was the particular protector of the canopic jar holding the stomach.

Nekhbet

Depicted as a vulture, Nekhbet was the chief goddess of Upper Egypt, whose king she protected. Her northern counterpart was Wadjet, the cobra goddess.

Her sanctuary stood at Nekheb, present-day El Kab, in Upper Egypt. Together with Wadjet, she formed the "Two Ladies" (Nebty), whose names appeared in one of the official titles of every pharaoh to symbolise the union of South and North. She was often depicted hovering with wings spread above the king's head to protect him in battle.

Nephthys

Nephthys is the daughter of Nut, the sister of Isis and the wife of Seth, the god of disorder. Yet her loyalty lies with Osiris, by whom she had a child, Anubis. When Seth discovered who the child's father was, he murdered Osiris, and Nephthys joined Isis in her search for Osiris's body. Like her sister Isis, she helps and protects the dead. She is shown as a woman wearing on her head the hieroglyph of her name.

Unlike her sister Isis, Nephthys never had a great temple of her own: her cult remained largely tied to Isis's, the two goddesses most often invoked together in funerary rites. Her name means "lady of the mansion," and she is one of the four protective goddesses of the canopic jars, watching over the one holding the lungs. She traditionally embodies the barren edge of the desert, in contrast to the fertile lands bordering the Nile that Isis represents.

Osiris

Osiris, one of the principal Egyptian gods, is first mentioned in the funerary texts of the Pyramid Age, when mummification began to be practised (around 2400 BC). He presided over the gods who decided the fate of kings at their death. He appears as a mummified man wearing a tall white crown flanked by two ostrich feathers. According to Egyptian mythology, Osiris was murdered by his brother Seth, then brought back to life through the love of his sister and wife, Isis. This myth describes the destructive forces that gave rise to the practice of mummification, while Isis's love symbolises regeneration and the promise of eternal life. The cycle of destruction, death and rebirth was repeated every year with the annual flooding of the Nile, the river that provided everything essential to life and gave birth to one of the first civilisations. Osiris is also identified with the miracle of the Nile and the rich harvests it made possible. Osiris, Isis and their son Horus formed a sacred family: god, goddess and divine child.

Wadjet

Wadjet was the cobra goddess whose deadly power protected the king of Lower Egypt. Her southern counterpart was Nekhbet, the vulture goddess.

Her cult centre, Buto, lay in the Nile delta. In her raised cobra form, the uraeus, she adorned the brow of pharaohs and gods alike as a symbol of sovereignty and an ultimate weapon of protection, ready to spit fire at anyone who threatened the king. She was also linked to the Eye of Re, the vengeful and protective aspect of the sun god.

Ptah

Creator god of the city of Memphis and husband of Sekhmet, Ptah is shown as a mummified man holding a was sceptre (a staff whose curved top is shaped like an animal's head and whose base is forked).

According to Memphite theology, engraved on the famous Shabaka Stone, Ptah created the world through the sheer power of his thought and speech alone — an act of intellectual creation unique within Egyptian mythology. With his wife Sekhmet and their son Nefertem, god of the lotus, he formed the Memphite triad; he was also the patron of craftsmen, sculptors, and builders, who regarded him as their supreme protector.

Re

The sun god was regarded as the central and original creative principle. The daily rising and setting of the sun gave tangible proof of the sun's power to sink into the western sky and be reborn each morning in the eastern sky. It was Re who gave the Egyptians the concept of Maat, the principle of truth (right) and fair justice. This fundamental concept became the cornerstone of Egyptian civilisation. The cosmic journey of the sun, symbolised by the scarab (the dung beetle that rolls the solar disc across the sky), would go on for as long as people continued to worship the sun god and Maat. When the other gods appeared, Re's supremacy was extended to other forms of the sun god: Shu, then Geb and finally Osiris. On earth, the kings of the Old Kingdom were regarded as the mortal incarnation of the sun god. In other words, the king was a god on earth, and through his just deeds he kept the world from falling into chaos. The sun god is also known as Re-Horakhty ("Horus of the horizon") and as Atum ("the All"), the substance from which all creation is drawn. Re-Horakhty is a god with a human body and a falcon's head, wearing a crown in the form of a solar disc encircled by a cobra, or a crown of ram's horns adorned with ostrich feathers. Atum is shown as King of Egypt and Lord of the universe, wearing the double crown of Egypt. All these forms of the sun god symbolise the promise of resurrection, an answer to the problem of human mortality. The cult of the sun was maintained by Egypt's kings for centuries. They built pyramids (symbolising a stairway climbing to the sun, or its slanting rays) and later temples dedicated to the sun gods. When a king died, his deeds were judged in the afterlife by Osiris, one of the forms of the sun god, who ruled the underworld. If his deeds were found just, the king was transformed into a form of the sun god.

Sekhmet

Sekhmet appears as a woman with the head of a lioness crowned by a solar disc and a uraeus (cobra). She was an important goddess of the Theban capital during the New Kingdom (1550-1070 BC). Her name means "the powerful one", and as such she embodies the aggressive side of female deities.

Her cult was closely tied to that of her husband Ptah at Memphis. As the "Eye of Re" sent to punish rebellious humanity, she is said, in the myth of the destruction of mankind, to have wrought such carnage that Re had to trick her by having beer dyed red spread across the land, which she mistook for blood: drunk, she fell asleep and abandoned her slaughter, revealing her dual nature as both a warrior and a healing goddess — Egyptian physicians indeed styled themselves "priests of Sekhmet."

Selkis

Selkis is usually shown as a woman with a scorpion on her head. Her name means "she who lets the throat breathe". She protects the falcon-headed canopic jar and, with three other goddesses, Isis, Nephthys and Neith, guards royal sarcophagi and canopic chests.

Selkis had no great temple of her own but played a leading funerary role alongside Isis, Nephthys, and Neith, each protecting one of the four canopic jars: she watched over the one holding the deceased's intestines. Her association with scorpions also made her a protector invoked against their stings, and her priests were said to be able to cure their venom.

Seth

Seth is the son of Geb and Nut. As the god of disorder, he is responsible for the murder of his brother Osiris. In the Egyptian dualist view of the cosmos, Seth was paired with Horus, the god who ruled the earth and ensured its order and stability. Seth has the rounded head of an animal, with tall, square-tipped ears and a tail standing straight up like an arrow. The animal he represents has never been identified. He is often shown with a human body and an elongated bird's head, which makes him look like the god Thoth.

His main cult centre stood at Ombos, in southern Egypt, where he was worshipped favourably for centuries: several pharaohs, such as Seti I, proudly bore his name, and the Hyksos identified him with their own storm god. Only from the Late Period onward, after repeated foreign occupations of Egypt, was Seth gradually vilified and associated with the outside enemy. Despite his reputation as a god of chaos, he paradoxically played an essential protective role: it was he who, aboard Re's solar barque, speared the serpent Apophis each night so the sun could continue its course.

Sobek

The name of this crocodile-headed god means "he who makes pregnant" or "he who makes fertile". At his place of worship in Kom Ombo, he shared a temple with Horus. He was later absorbed into the cult of Amun and worshipped as a manifestation of the sun god, Re.

His two great cult centres were Kom Ombo, where he shared a temple with Horus, and the Faiyum, a marshy region nicknamed "the land of Sobek," where sacred crocodiles were raised, adorned with jewellery, and mummified upon death in his honour. The Middle Kingdom pharaoh Amenemhat III held him in particular devotion, multiplying sanctuaries to him throughout the Faiyum, seeing in the crocodile god an embodiment of royal power and mastery over the waters of the Nile.

Sokar

Sokar is the falcon-headed god of the Memphite necropolis, depicted as a man with a falcon's head wearing the atef crown. An ancient funerary deity, he oversaw the proper conduct of embalming rites and protected the craftsmen who worked the precious metals destined for the tombs.

His cult, celebrated each year during the Feast of Sokar at Memphis, included a procession in which his sacred barque, the Henu, was dragged through the city before being hauled up a sand mound symbolising the sun rising over the necropolis. Over time, Sokar merged with Ptah and Osiris to form the composite deity Ptah-Sokar-Osiris, embodying creation, death, and resurrection all at once.

Thoth

Thoth is the messenger of the sun god. He is the god of knowledge and wisdom, the inventor of writing and science, and the protector of scribes. He is shown as a man with the head of an ibis, as a baboon, or as the moon.

His great cult centre, Hermopolis, made him the demiurge of the Hermopolitan cosmogony. As the inventor of hieroglyphic writing and mathematics, he acted as scribe and arbiter of the gods: he was the one who recorded the verdict at the weighing of the heart, who ruled in Horus' favour in his conflict with Seth, and who, according to the Osirian myth, saved the young Horus after the scorpion's sting.

Tueris

A goddess who protected women in childbirth, Tueris is shown with the head of a hippopotamus, the legs of a lion, and the back and tail of a crocodile. Her heavy breasts and swollen belly show that she is pregnant.

Like Bes, with whom she often shared the same protective role, Tueris never had a great state temple: she was worshipped in the domestic sphere, in the form of amulets and figurines placed in birthing rooms to ward off the evil forces threatening mother and child during birth.

Wepwawet

Wepwawet, "opener of the ways," is a jackal- or wolf-headed god whose cult centre lay at Asyut, in Upper Egypt. Originally a war god, he symbolically opened the way for the pharaoh during battles and royal processions, his standard carried at the head of the procession.

Wepwawet also played an essential funerary role: it was he who opened the deceased's path into the afterlife, a role that gradually drew him closer to Anubis, to the point that the two canine-headed gods were sometimes confused with one another. His statue also accompanied the pharaoh during the coronation ceremony, leading the way to the throne.
