The Egyptian pantheon counts hundreds of deities. It can seem bewildering, but it follows a few principles:
  • the same god can take on several appearances: an animal, a human being, or a human body topped by an animal head. These forms do not exclude one another: each shows one aspect of the god’s nature;
  • animals are symbols: the falcon evokes the sky and kingship, the jackal the cemeteries, the lioness strength and anger;
  • most gods were first attached to one city, before their cult sometimes spread to the whole of Egypt;
  • gods can merge to form a new one, like Amun and Re, who became Amun-Re.
The gods were present at every moment of daily life: people looked to them for protection, healing and prosperity, and prayed to them as much in the great temples as in the privacy of their homes.
Here are the main deities of the Egyptian pantheon.

Amemet

Amemet, “the Devourer”, is a composite creature: she has the head of a crocodile, the forequarters of a lion and the hindquarters of a hippopotamus, the three animals the Egyptians feared most. At the judgement of the dead, she stands by the scale on which the heart of the deceased is weighed. If that heart, weighed down by wrongdoing, outweighs the feather of Maat, she devours it.

Amemet never had a temple or a cult: she was feared, not prayed to. She exists only in scenes of the judgement, such as those of the Book of the Dead. Being devoured by her did not mean dying, but vanishing for ever, deprived of any life in the afterlife: the “second death” that every Egyptian dreaded.

Amun

Amun, whose name means “the hidden one”, is the great god of Thebes. He is shown as a man wearing a crown of two tall plumes, sometimes in the form of a ram with curved horns or of a goose. With his wife Mut and their son Khonsu, he forms the Theban triad.

At first a simple local god, Amun followed the rise of Thebes. In the New Kingdom, he became the god of the state and the “king of the gods”, to whom the pharaohs credited their victories. His temple at Karnak became the largest in Egypt, and his priesthood one of the richest and most powerful in the land. United with the sun god, he was worshipped under the name Amun-Re.

Amun-Re

Amun-Re was born of the merging of two gods: Amun, “the hidden one”, god of Thebes, and Re, the sun, god of Heliopolis. He unites what each of them stands for: an invisible power, present everywhere, and the dazzling light that gives life to the world. He is shown like Amun, as a man wearing two tall plumes, often topped by the sun disc.

Re had been the great sun god since the Old Kingdom. When kings from Thebes took power in the Middle Kingdom, their god Amun was joined with Re to give him national standing. In the New Kingdom, Amun-Re, “king of the gods”, became the supreme god of Egypt, creator of the world and father of the pharaoh.

Anubis

Anubis is the jackal-headed god who watches over the dead. Jackals prowled around cemeteries, on the edge of the desert: the Egyptians made this animal the guardian of tombs, so that it would protect them instead of desecrating them. His black colour evokes the fertile soil of the Nile and rebirth, not mourning.

It was he who embalmed the body of Osiris, which made him the patron of embalmers: during mummifications, the priest who led the ritual wore his mask. He then guides the deceased through the afterlife and, at the judgement, adjusts the scale on which the heart is weighed against the feather of Maat. According to the most widespread tradition, he is the son of Osiris and Nephthys, raised by Isis.

Apophis

Apophis, or Apep, is a gigantic serpent who embodies chaos. He existed before Creation, in the waters of the Nun, and was never created: he therefore cannot be destroyed. Every night, he attacks the bark of Re during its crossing of the underworld, to swallow the sun and plunge the world back into darkness. Every night, the gods of the bark drive him back, but never for good.

Apophis had neither temple nor cult: he stood for everything Egyptian religion sought to fight. In the temples, priests recited a ritual, the “Book of Overthrowing Apophis”, to help Re in his struggle: they made a wax figurine of the serpent, then trampled, pierced and burned it. This endless fight was a reminder that the order of the world, Maat, always had to be defended against chaos.

Aten

The Aten is the sun disc itself, the visible form of the sun. Unlike most Egyptian gods, he has neither a human body nor an animal head: he is shown as a disc whose rays end in small hands, holding out the sign of life, the ankh, to the king and his family. Not to be confused with Atum, the creator of the Heliopolitan cosmogony.

Long a mere aspect of the sun god, the Aten met a unique fate in the 14th century BC. The pharaoh Amenhotep IV made him the only god of his kingdom and took the name Akhenaten, “he who is useful to the Aten”. He closed the temples of the other gods, had the name of Amun hammered out and founded a new capital devoted to the Aten, Akhetaten, present-day Amarna. This religious revolution did not outlive him: from the reign of Tutankhamun, the old gods were restored and Amarna abandoned.

Atum

Atum is the creator of the Heliopolitan cosmogony. His name means both “the Complete One” and “the One who does not yet exist”: before Creation, he held within himself everything that was to be. He rose alone from the waters of the Nun, stood on the primordial mound and drew out of himself the first divine couple, Shu and Tefnut, from whom all the gods of the Ennead descend.

Atum is shown as a bearded man, most often wearing the double crown of Upper and Lower Egypt, since he is the king of the universe, sometimes simply the divine wig. He is also the evening sun, the old man who sets in the west, which explains why he was united with Re under the name Re-Atum. His sacred animals were the serpent, the eel and the ichneumon (the mongoose). One text announces that at the end of time, Atum will destroy everything he has created and become a serpent again, alone in the waters of the Nun.

Bastet

Bastet is shown as a cat or as a cat-headed woman, often holding a sistrum, a sacred musical instrument. Goddess of the home, of joy and of motherhood, she protects households and pregnant women. Yet she was originally a lioness goddess, as fearsome as Sekhmet: the cat is her calmed, gentle and protective form.

Her main cult centre was Bubastis, in the Delta, where her great temple stood. The Greek historian Herodotus describes a yearly festival there that drew hundreds of thousands of pilgrims, amid music, dancing and wine. Cats were bred there in her honour, then mummified and offered to the goddess by the thousand.

Bes

Bes is a bearded dwarf with a grimacing, lion-like face, who often sticks out his tongue. He wears a crown of feathers and sometimes brandishes knives or a tambourine. His frightening appearance is not meant to scare people, but to drive away demons, snakes and evil spirits.

Bes never had a great temple: he was a household god, and a very popular one. His image decorated beds, headrests, mirrors and amulets. He protected sleep, women in childbirth and newborn babies, often alongside the goddess Tueris. He was also linked with music, dancing and festivities.

Geb & Nut

Geb is the earth, Nut the sky. Children of Shu and Tefnut, they are both brother and sister and husband and wife. Geb is shown lying on the ground, sometimes with green skin like the plants that grow on him, with a goose on his head, the sign that writes his name. Earthquakes were said to be his laughter.

Nut is a woman with an arched body covered in stars, who touches the earth with the tips of her fingers and toes. Every evening she swallows the sun, which passes through her body during the night, and gives birth to it in the morning. For this reason, her image was often painted on the inside of coffin lids: like the sun, the deceased would be reborn from her body. Geb and Nut are the parents of Osiris, Horus the Elder, Seth, Isis and Nephthys.

Hapi

Hapi is the god of the Nile flood. Every summer, the river overflowed and covered the fields with fertile silt, without which nothing could have grown in Egypt. Hapi is the embodiment of this vital phenomenon. He is shown as a pot-bellied man with heavy breasts, crowned with water plants and bearing offerings: these generous forms express the abundance he brings.

The flood was believed to gush from caverns near the First Cataract, on the southern border of Egypt. Hapi had no great temple, but hymns were sung to him and offerings thrown into the river to ensure a flood neither too low, which meant famine, nor too high, which meant destruction. He is sometimes shown as a pair, with the plants of the South and those of the North, to symbolise the union of the two halves of Egypt.

Hathor

Hathor, whose name means “the house of Horus”, is one of the best-loved goddesses of Egypt. She appears as a cow, as a woman with cow’s ears, or as a woman wearing cow horns around the sun disc. Goddess of love, beauty, music, dance and joyful drunkenness, she is also the protector of women and the “lady of the West”, who welcomes the dead into the Theban mountain.

Daughter of Re, she is also the Eye of Re, and her gentleness hides a terrible side: in the myth of the destruction of humankind, it is she who, changed into Sekhmet, slaughtered the rebellious humans. She is the wife of Horus of Edfu and the symbolic mother of the king. Her great temple stands at Dendera, where her reunion with her husband was celebrated every year.

Heka

Heka is the personification of magic, the force that allowed the creator to give shape to the world. For the Egyptians, magic was in no way forbidden: it was a divine power, used by gods, priests and physicians to protect, heal and ward off evil. Heka himself claimed to have existed before all the other gods.

Heka travels in the bark of Re with Sia, knowledge, and Hu, creative speech: together, the three of them stand for the forces with which the sun creates and protects the world. At Esna, he was worshipped as a divine child, son of Khnum and Neith, shown with a finger raised to his mouth, like young children.

Horus

Horus is the falcon god, whose name means “the one above” or “the distant one”, like the bird of prey soaring high in the sky. His right eye was the sun, his left eye the moon. He is shown as a falcon or as a falcon-headed man wearing the double crown of Upper and Lower Egypt. He is the son of Isis and Osiris, not to be confused with his uncle Horus the Elder.

Born in secret in the marshes of the Delta, where his mother hid him from Seth, Horus grew up to avenge his father. After a long struggle against Seth, he was recognised by the gods as the rightful heir to the throne of Egypt. He thus became the god of kingship: every pharaoh reigned as a “living Horus”. The Eye of Horus, torn out by Seth and then healed, became one of the most widespread symbols of protection. His great temple stands at Edfu.

Horus the Elder

Horus the Elder, whom the Greeks called Haroeris, is one of the five children of Geb and Nut, born on the second of the epagomenal days. He is a falcon god of the sky, whose eyes are the sun and the moon. He must not be confused with Horus, the son of Isis and Osiris: the Egyptians told the two apart, even if their features often ended up blending.

A sky god and a warrior, Horus the Elder is shown as a falcon-headed man or as a falcon-headed sphinx wearing the double crown. He was worshipped at Kom Ombo, in a double temple half of which he shared with the crocodile god Sobek, and in the Delta, at Letopolis.

Isis

Isis is the sister and wife of Osiris, and the mother of Horus. She wears on her head the hieroglyph of the throne, which writes her name; from the New Kingdom onwards, she also wears cow horns around the sun disc, like Hathor. She is often shown nursing her son or mourning her husband, her wings spread.

A faithful wife and protective mother, Isis gathered the dismembered body of Osiris and protected Horus until he won back the throne. She is also the most powerful of magicians, the “great of magic”, who wrested his secret name from Re. Her great temple at Philae, in the south of Egypt, stayed open until the 6th century, long after the other cults had ended. Her cult spread throughout the Graeco-Roman world, as far as Rome and the edges of the Empire.

Khepri

Khepri is the rising sun. He is shown as a scarab or as a man whose head is a scarab. The Egyptians watched the dung beetle pushing its ball of dung before it, from which its young seemed to emerge: they saw in it the image of the sun pushed across the sky every morning and of a life that renews itself.

His name comes from the verb kheper, “to come into being, to transform”. Khepri is the form Re takes at daybreak, before becoming Re at noon and Atum in the evening. A symbol of rebirth, the scarab was one of the most widespread amulets in Egypt: one was placed over the heart of mummies to ensure the deceased a new life.

Khnum

Khnum is a ram-headed god with long, horizontal, wavy horns. He is the “divine potter”: he is shown shaping children and their ka, their life force, on his wheel, before placing them in their mother’s womb. Some texts even credit him with the creation of gods and humans.

Khnum was also the lord of the Nile flood, which he released from caverns beneath the First Cataract. His main sanctuary was on the island of Elephantine, on the southern border of Egypt, where he formed a triad with the goddesses Satet and Anuket. He was also worshipped at Esna, where his temple of the Roman period still stands.

Khonsu

Khonsu, “the traveller”, is a moon god who crosses the night sky. Son of Amun and Mut, he forms the Theban triad with them. He is shown as a young boy, with the sidelock of hair that Egyptian children wore, or as a falcon-headed man. Either way, he wears a crescent moon topped by the lunar disc.

God of time and of the months, Khonsu was also known for driving out evil spirits and healing the sick. A stela tells that one of his statues was sent all the way to the distant land of Bakhtan to free a princess possessed by a demon. His temple still stands within the great complex of Karnak, at Thebes.

Maat

Maat is shown as a woman wearing an ostrich feather on her head. Daughter of Re, she is much more than a goddess: she is the order of the world as the creator established it. Maat is at once truth, justice, balance and the regular course of things: the rising of the sun, the return of the flood, respect for the law.

The pharaoh’s first duty was to make Maat prevail and to drive back her opposite, chaos. In the temples, the king offered the gods a statuette of Maat, to show that he was fulfilling this mission. Every Egyptian also had to “live by Maat”: at the judgement of the dead, the heart of the deceased was weighed against the goddess’s feather.

Min

Min is the god of fertility. He is shown standing, his body tightly wrapped like a mummy, with an erect phallus, wearing two tall plumes, his right arm raised holding a flail. Lettuces often grow behind him, their white sap being associated with semen.

He was worshipped at Akhmim and at Coptos, the starting point of the eastern desert tracks towards the Red Sea: he also protected caravans and prospectors. At the start of the harvest, the pharaoh himself took part in his great festival, during which he cut a sheaf of wheat. Min was later associated with Amun, whose creative power he embodied.

Montu

Montu is the god of war. He is shown as a falcon-headed man wearing the sun disc and two tall plumes, sometimes as a bull-headed man, the animal that embodied his strength. Pharaohs compared themselves to him in battle, calling themselves “mighty as Montu”.

Montu was originally the great god of the Theban region, long before the rise of Amun. Several kings of the 11th Dynasty bore his name, Mentuhotep, “Montu is satisfied”. He was worshipped at Medamud, Armant and Tod, and had his own temple within the Karnak complex.

Mut

Mut, whose name means “the mother”, is the wife of Amun and the mother of Khonsu, with whom she forms the Theban triad. She appears as a woman wearing the double crown of Egypt or a vulture headdress, the bird that writes her name, a symbol of motherhood.

Her temple, south of the great temple of Amun at Karnak, was surrounded by a crescent-shaped sacred lake. The symbolic mother of the pharaoh, Mut could also be fearsome, like Sekhmet: King Amenhotep III had hundreds of statues of the lioness goddess placed in her temple to soothe her anger.

Nefertem

Nefertem is the god of the blue lotus and of perfumes. He is shown as a young man wearing a lotus flower in full bloom on his head, sometimes topped by two tall plumes. At Memphis, he is the son of Ptah and Sekhmet, and forms the Memphite triad with them.

According to a creation myth, a giant lotus rose from the waters of the Nun on the first morning of the world. As it opened, it revealed the young sun. Nefertem embodies this flower, which opens at dawn and closes in the evening, and the fragrance it gives off, a symbol of rebirth. Like his mother, he could also appear in the form of a lion.

Neith

Neith is one of the oldest deities of Egypt, worshipped even before the country was unified. Goddess of war and hunting, her emblem is a bow and two crossed arrows. She is also the patron of weaving. She is shown wearing the red crown of Lower Egypt.

Her cult centre was Sais, in the Delta, which became the capital of Egypt under the 26th Dynasty. At Esna, she was regarded as a creator, born of herself before all the gods and mother of Re. In funerary rites, she watched over the canopic jars with Isis, Nephthys and Selkis, and protected in particular the one holding the stomach of the deceased.

Nekhbet

Nekhbet is the vulture goddess, protector of Upper Egypt. She is shown as a vulture with outspread wings, hovering above the king to protect him, or as a woman wearing the white crown of the South. Her sanctuary was at Nekheb, present-day El Kab.

With Wadjet, the cobra goddess of Lower Egypt, she formed the “Two Ladies”, who symbolised the union of South and North. One of the pharaoh’s five official names was placed under their protection, and their two heads, vulture and cobra, often adorned the king’s brow, as on the mask of Tutankhamun.

Nephthys

Nephthys, daughter of Geb and Nut, is the sister of Isis and the wife of Seth. Her name means “the lady of the house”, and she wears on her head the hieroglyphs that write it: a house topped by a basket. According to one tradition, she bore Osiris a son, Anubis.

Despite her marriage to Seth, Nephthys took the side of Osiris. After her brother’s murder, she helped Isis find and gather his body, and the two sisters, changed into kites, mourned him together. They thus became the protectors of the dead. Nephthys watched in particular over the canopic jar that held the lungs of the deceased. She had no great temple of her own, and her cult remained bound up with that of Isis.

Osiris

Osiris is the ruler of the world of the dead. He is shown as a mummy, his skin green like vegetation or black like the fertile soil, wearing the atef crown, a tall white mitre flanked by two feathers. He holds the crook and the flail, the insignia of kingship.

The eldest son of Geb and Nut, he was first a benevolent king who taught humans farming. Murdered by his brother Seth, he was brought back to life by his wife Isis, then reigned over the afterlife, where he judges the dead. A god who died and rose again, he also embodied the vegetation that is reborn after each flood. Every Egyptian hoped to share his fate and rise again like him. His great place of worship was Abydos, where his mysteries were celebrated every year.

Ptah

Ptah is the great god of Memphis. He is shown as a man with his body wrapped like a mummy, wearing a blue skullcap and a straight beard. He holds a sceptre that combines three symbols: the was sceptre (power), the djed pillar (stability) and the ankh (life).

According to Memphite theology, carved on the Shabaka Stone, Ptah created the world through the thought of his heart and the word of his tongue. With his wife Sekhmet and their son Nefertem, he formed the triad of Memphis, where the Apis bull was regarded as his living incarnation. He was also the patron of craftsmen, sculptors and architects.

Re

Re, or Ra, is the sun, the god whose daily course gives life to the world. He is most often shown as a falcon-headed man crowned with the sun disc encircled by a cobra: he then bears the name Re-Horakhty, “Re, Horus of the two horizons”. His great cult centre was Heliopolis.

Re changes form as the day goes by: he is Khepri, the scarab, at sunrise, Re at noon and Atum, the old man, at sunset. The Great Sphinx of Giza was worshipped as Harmachis, “Horus in the horizon”, another of his forms. Re travels by day across the sky aboard his bark, and by night through the underworld, where he faces the serpent Apophis. In the New Kingdom, it was even said that his soul united every night with the body of Osiris, to be reborn in the morning.

From the 5th Dynasty on, every pharaoh bore the title “son of Re”. Kings built him open-air sun temples, dominated by an obelisk, and joined his cult with that of other great gods: Amun-Re at Thebes, Sobek-Re in the Faiyum.

Satet & Anuket

Satet and Anuket are the two goddesses of the First Cataract of the Nile, on the southern border of Egypt. With the god Khnum, they formed the triad of the island of Elephantine, which watched over the source of the flood. Satet wears the white crown of Upper Egypt flanked by two antelope horns; Anuket wears a tall crown of feathers.

Satet, “she who pours”, was charged with pouring the cool waters of the flood over Egypt. Anuket, goddess of the rapids and of the border region, was sometimes regarded as her daughter. During the flood, Anuket was celebrated by throwing coins, gold and jewellery into the river.

Sekhmet

Sekhmet, “the powerful one”, is a lioness-headed goddess crowned with the sun disc and the cobra. She embodies the destructive force of the scorching sun and divine anger. Wife of Ptah and mother of Nefertem, she was worshipped at Memphis. At Thebes, King Amenhotep III dedicated hundreds of statues to her.

As the Eye of Re, she was sent to punish the rebellious humans and nearly wiped out humankind, until the gods made her drunk on red-dyed beer. She was held responsible for epidemics, but she could also cure them: her priests were famed for their medical knowledge, and Egyptian physicians placed themselves under her protection.

Selkis

Selkis, or Serket, is shown as a woman with a scorpion on her head. Her name means “she who makes the throat breathe”: she protected against scorpion stings and snake bites, which cause choking. Her priests were healers, specialists in venoms.

Selkis also had an important funerary role. With Isis, Nephthys and Neith, she watched over the canopic jars, and protected in particular the one holding the intestines of the deceased. One of the finest images of the goddess is the gilded statuette that guards, arms outstretched, the canopic shrine of Tutankhamun. In the bark of Re, it is she who pins down Apophis.

Seshat

Seshat is the goddess of writing, reckoning and records, companion or daughter of Thoth. She is shown as a woman dressed in a leopard skin, wearing a seven-pointed star topped by a bow, and holding the reed pen and palette of the scribe.

Seshat kept the record of the pharaohs’ regnal years, writing their names on the leaves of the sacred tree of Heliopolis. She also presided over the founding of temples: during the ritual of “stretching the cord”, she helped the king pull taut the rope that fixed the exact site of the future sanctuary.

Seth

Seth, son of Geb and Nut, is the god of the desert, of storms and of disorder. He is shown with the head of a strange animal, with a long curved snout and upright ears squared off at the top, which has never been identified with certainty. He is associated with the colour red and with the arid lands that surround the Nile valley.

Seth is best known for murdering his brother Osiris to take his throne, before being defeated by Horus. Yet he was not always a cursed god. Worshipped at Ombos, in Upper Egypt, he was long respected for his strength: the pharaohs Seti I and Seti II bore his name, and it is he who, every night, defends the bark of Re against Apophis. Only at the end of Egyptian history, after foreign invasions, did he become the god of evil and of Egypt’s enemies.

Shu & Tefnut

Shu and Tefnut are the first divine couple, the children Atum drew out of himself at the beginning of Creation. Shu is the god of air and light, often shown wearing an ostrich feather, which writes his name. Tefnut, his sister and wife, is the goddess of moisture; she appears as a lioness-headed woman. Together, they gave birth to Geb, the earth, and Nut, the sky.

Shu’s most famous role is to have separated his children: standing on Geb, he holds up the body of Nut at arm’s length, creating the space in which living beings breathe. Tefnut, for her part, is the heroine of a myth in which, angry with her father, she fled into the Nubian desert in the form of a lioness, before being brought back to Egypt, calmed.

Sobek

Sobek is the crocodile god, shown as a crocodile or as a crocodile-headed man. Lord of waters and marshes, he embodied the fearsome power of the Nile, but also the fertility it brings. People prayed to him for protection from crocodiles, which were very real in the river.

His two great cult centres were Kom Ombo, where he shared a double temple with Horus the Elder, and the Faiyum, where sacred crocodiles adorned with jewels were kept and mummified when they died. The Middle Kingdom pharaoh Amenemhat III particularly favoured his cult in that region. Associated with the sun, Sobek was also worshipped under the name Sobek-Re.

Sokar

Sokar is a falcon god, lord of the necropolis of Memphis, which the site of Saqqara is thought to be named after. He is shown as a falcon-headed man, sometimes mummified, wearing the atef crown. A god of the dead and of the underworld, he also protected the craftsmen who worked precious metals for the tombs.

During his great festival at Memphis, his sacred bark, the Henu, was carried in procession around the walls of the city. Sokar was closely associated with Ptah, the god of Memphis, and with Osiris, the ruler of the dead, until the three formed a single god, Ptah-Sokar-Osiris, who brought together creation, death and rebirth.

Thoth

Thoth is the god of wisdom, writing and the moon. He is shown as an ibis-headed man holding the reed pen and palette of the scribe, or in the form of a baboon. He was credited with inventing hieroglyphs, mathematics and the calendar: he was the patron of scribes and scholars.

Scribe and messenger of the gods, he records the verdict at the weighing of the heart, advises the tribunal that decided between Horus and Seth, and heals the young Horus stung by a scorpion. His great cult centre was Hermopolis, where he held a central place in the creation of the world. The Greeks identified him with their god Hermes.

Tueris

Tueris, or Taweret, “the great one”, is a hippopotamus goddess. She stands upright, with the belly of a pregnant woman and heavy breasts, the paws of a lion and the back of a crocodile. This fearsome mix allowed her to frighten off the forces of evil.

Tueris protected pregnant women, childbirth and newborn babies. Like Bes, with whom she is often associated, she had no great temple: she was worshipped at home, in the form of amulets, figurines and images painted on beds and everyday objects.

Wadjet

Wadjet is the cobra goddess, protector of Lower Egypt. Her name means “the green one”, like the papyrus of the Delta. Her sanctuary was at Buto, in the Delta. With Nekhbet, the vulture goddess of Upper Egypt, she formed the “Two Ladies”, protectors of the king.

In the form of a rearing cobra, the uraeus, she sits on the brow of pharaohs and gods, ready to spit her burning venom at their enemies. She is thus associated with the Eye of Re, the protective and avenging power of the sun god.

Wepwawet

Wepwawet, “the opener of the ways”, is a wolf or jackal god. His cult centre was Asyut, in Upper Egypt, which the Greeks called Lycopolis, “the city of the wolf”. A warrior god, he cleared the way for the king: his standard was carried at the head of royal processions and armies.

He also opened the way of the dead to the afterlife. At Abydos, he led the procession of Osiris during his mysteries. This funerary role brought him close to Anubis, the other canine god, so much so that the two gods were sometimes confused.
